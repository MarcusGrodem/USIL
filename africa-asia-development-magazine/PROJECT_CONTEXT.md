# Project Context

This document is the long-term memory for the project. It reconstructs the current project from the conversation and should allow a new team member or AI assistant to continue without reading the original chat.

## Background

The team selected the assignment topic:

> Economic development in Africa: Why this region could not grow like the Asia region. Cultural analysis.

The original wording is broad and risks treating two diverse continents as single units. The project was therefore reframed as an investigation of why many African economies followed different development paths from several East and Southeast Asian economies, and what role culture played alongside history, institutions, geography, trade and policy.

## Assignment requirements

The teacher requires:

- an analytical report;
- magazine style;
- at least 40 pages;
- sources;
- preparation for a presentation;

The work will be assessed on:

- innovation in the report.

The teacher clarified that the page count does not mean 40 pages of dense writing. Pages may use images, graphics, videos, games, interactive activities and other magazine devices.

Later teacher feedback added two requirements:

1. The six Hofstede dimensions should be considered.
2. The team should develop its own theories rather than only applying existing frameworks.

## Project objectives

The project should:

- explain the economic divergence without reducing it to culture;
- use culture as one part of a wider causal system;
- show how colonialism changed states, borders, infrastructure, education and trade;
- compare real countries rather than only regional averages;
- develop original, testable group theories;
- actively challenge those theories with counterexamples;
- create a memorable visual and interactive experience;
- provide enough structure that any team member can present the argument.

## Central question

The primary question is:

> Why did many African and Asian economies follow different development paths, and how much can culture explain?

The central tension is:

> Was slower growth caused by culture, or was culture itself partly shaped by history and institutions?

The project should not promise one simple answer. Its strength is showing how several mechanisms interact.

## Current concept

The magazine is structured as an investigation.

The reader begins with the apparent economic divergence, learns why broad continental comparisons are risky, explores colonial inheritances and East Asian industrialisation, encounters cultural dimensions, tests three group theories, considers counterexamples and ends with a conditional synthesis.

The magazine should repeatedly ask the reader to decide before revealing the analysis. This may happen through a poll, a game, a ranking task, a map exercise, a class survey or a provocative statement.

## Current analytical direction

The working argument is:

> Asia's faster development in selected cases cannot be explained by culture alone. Industrial policy, institutions, trade, infrastructure, education, geopolitics and historical conditions mattered greatly. Cultural factors such as trust, authority, family networks and attitudes toward long-term investment may have affected how institutions worked and how reforms were implemented. Those cultural patterns were also shaped by history and institutions.

This is a working direction, not a final conclusion.

## Economic backbone

The report needs an economic foundation before cultural interpretation.

The key economic concept is structural transformation: moving workers, firms and capital from low-productivity activities into higher-productivity sectors. The planned contrast is that several East Asian economies industrialised through manufacturing, exports, technology learning and firm upgrading, while structural change in many African economies was slower or moved labour toward lower-productivity services rather than manufacturing.

The main indicators proposed in the discussion are:

- income per person;
- labour productivity;
- employment by sector;
- manufacturing share;
- export composition;
- literacy and education;
- urbanisation;
- infrastructure and electricity;
- firm size and formality;
- trust and institutional confidence where credible data exist.

## Colonialism as a central bridge

Colonialism is not treated only as historical background. It is a bridge between the economic and cultural analysis.

The project will examine:

- extractive trade structures;
- infrastructure built from mines or plantations to ports;
- borders that divided or combined political and ethnic communities;
- colonial bureaucracies designed to control territory rather than deliver broad public services;
- education systems and administrative languages;
- indirect rule and the use of local authorities;
- centralised administration and assimilation;
- the different inheritances available to governments at independence.

British and French rule will be presented as broad tendencies, not universal templates.

- British rule is associated in the draft with indirect rule and governance through local authorities.
- French rule is associated with more centralised administration, language and assimilation.

The project must show exceptions and avoid claiming that every colony experienced the same system.

Belgian, Portuguese, German and Italian colonial cases are included briefly to prevent the analysis from becoming a British/French binary.

## Asia comparison

Asia was also colonised, so the project cannot argue that colonialism alone explains the divergence.

Examples mentioned in the discussion include:

- India under Britain;
- Vietnam under France;
- Indonesia under the Netherlands;
- Korea under Japan;
- Malaysia under Britain.

The better question is why colonial legacies interacted differently with post-independence institutions, economic policy, geopolitics and culture.

The East Asian development section should examine:

- mass or broad education;
- infrastructure and power;
- export manufacturing;
- state coordination;
- firm learning;
- reinvestment;
- technology upgrading;
- movement into higher-value production.

The report must not present one universal "Asian model." Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, China and Vietnam used different mixes of markets, state direction, external support and global trade.

## Cultural analysis

Culture is treated as a lens and possible mechanism, not as a verdict on a country.

The teacher specifically highlighted Hofstede's six dimensions:

1. Power distance
2. Individualism versus collectivism
3. Uncertainty avoidance
4. Masculinity versus femininity
5. Long-term versus short-term orientation
6. Indulgence versus restraint

The current plan gives more depth to:

- trust, although trust is not one of Hofstede's six dimensions;
- individualism versus collectivism;
- power distance;
- long-term orientation.

The remaining three Hofstede dimensions receive shorter treatment unless research shows that one is especially relevant.

Hofstede country scores must be used cautiously. They describe average survey patterns and do not define every person. They should not be used to claim that a cultural score directly caused national growth.

Other concepts considered include:

- universalism versus particularism from Trompenaars;
- high-context versus low-context communication from Hall;
- personal versus institutional trust;
- in-group and out-group behaviour;
- family and kinship obligations.

These are secondary lenses. The final magazine should not become a catalogue of cultural frameworks.

## Original group theories

The teacher asked the group to develop its own theories. An early draft proposed five theories, but the user decided that this made the magazine too theory-heavy. The current direction uses three recurring working theories, introduced first as questions.

### 1. Connected Development Theory

Question:

> What if the decisive difference was not the amount of infrastructure, but whom it connected?

Working claim:

Colonial export corridors often connected resources to ports. Development may require networks that connect workers, firms, cities and regional markets to one another.

### 2. Radius of Trust Theory

Question:

> How far beyond family and close networks can economic trust travel?

Working claim:

Larger firms and formal markets may grow more easily when trust extends into contracts, courts, banks and professional management. Historical shocks and weak institutions may restrict this radius, while strong institutions may extend it.

### 3. Continuity + Adaptation Theory

Question:

> Can a country learn industrially if its direction changes every few years?

Working claim:

Industrial capability may need long-term policy continuity. Continuity alone is not enough; governments also need feedback and the ability to correct policies that fail.

These are original working hypotheses for the project. The final report must state predictions, evidence, challenges and limitations for each theory.

## Case strategy

The current cases have different jobs in the argument.

### South Korea

Used to illustrate war recovery, export orientation, industrial policy, education, firm growth and movement into higher-value production.

### Ghana

Used as the main paired comparison with South Korea. Ghana offers a post-independence African case involving commodity dependence, state-led projects, political changes and later reforms.

The comparison is useful but imperfect. It must not be presented as a controlled experiment.

### Botswana

Used as a counterexample to the claim that a single "African culture" blocks development. The exact indicators and argument still need to be researched and defined.

### Second African case

Not yet decided. Candidates mentioned are Mauritius, Morocco, Kenya, Rwanda and Ethiopia. The case should add a different pathway rather than simply provide another success ranking.

### Variation within Asia

The project should compare fast, middle and slower development paths in Asia. The final countries have not been selected.

## Content and layout direction

The magazine should use:

- full-page section openers;
- split-screen comparisons;
- mirrored dashboards with identical scales;
- maps and timelines;
- short analytical copy;
- large questions and pull quotes;
- flow diagrams and capability loops;
- case-study timelines;
- evidence-versus-challenge boxes;
- counterexample pages;
- visible source notes;
- QR codes only when the linked content adds genuine value.

Pages should not be filled with text simply to reach the page count. Approximately 15-18 pages may contain substantial written analysis, while other pages can carry visuals, interactions, maps, cases and summaries.

## Important reasoning principles

1. Do not treat continents as cultures.
2. Do not confuse correlation with causation.
3. Show two-way influence between culture and institutions.
4. Use country cases and time periods precisely.
5. Give counterexamples a real role in changing the argument.
6. Do not assume British colonies always performed better than French colonies.
7. Do not claim that colonialism explains everything because parts of Asia were also colonised.
8. Do not treat Hofstede scores as individual traits.
9. Do not present the team's theories as established literature.
10. Mark all unfinished data and citations clearly.

The teacher encouraged a creative magazine rather than a conventional written report. This led to interactive games, QR-linked activities, visual cases and experiments.

An initial revision proposed five theories. The user rejected a theory-heavy approach and asked for two or three theories, supported by questions and statements that make the reader think. The current version uses three.
