## Editorial answer in one paragraph

The defensible explanation is not that a single “Asian culture” produced growth while a single “African culture” prevented it. South Korea combined unusually rapid capability building, high investment, agricultural and land change, basic education, infrastructure, access to foreign technology, and state support that was often tied to export performance; it also benefited from postwar aid, procurement, security ties, and expanding world markets. Ghana experienced substantial structural change, especially movement out of agriculture, but much of the shift went into services, construction, and mining rather than sustained manufacturing deepening. That difference helps explain divergence, but it is not a continent-wide law: Mauritius built an African export-and-services diversification path, Botswana achieved large income gains while remaining diamond-dependent, Malaysia industrialised through multinational-led electronics, and the Philippines became a major electronics exporter without matching Korea’s depth or income trajectory. The evidence therefore supports a conditional, sequenced explanation centred on productive capabilities and institutions under particular international conditions—not cultural destiny (McMillan et al., 2014, p. 11; Rodrik, 1995, abstract; World Bank, 1993, pp. 4–7, 20–26, 79–80, 127–130).

## 1. What the five measures mean—and why they must not be collapsed

Structural transformation means **workers and economic activity moving from lower-productivity activities into higher-productivity ones, while productivity also improves inside sectors**. The first part is a between-sector effect; the second is a within-sector effect. A country can move workers out of agriculture without building a large manufacturing base, and it can raise income through minerals or high-value services without following Korea’s route (McMillan et al., 2014, p. 11; World Bank, 2020, pp. 1, 5–7).

**Interpretive rule:** none of these is a synonym for the others. The magazine should show their relationship, then state the remaining causal uncertainty.

### 2.2 What the pair supports

Ghana did transform: between 1991 and 2020, agricultural employment fell 34.4 percentage points, while services rose 28.2 points and industry rose 6.2 points. But Ghana’s MVA share changed little between the 1965 proxy and 2020. A World Bank decomposition describes the country’s transformation as movement mainly toward services, construction, and mining, with manufacturing underperforming and many new service activities remaining relatively low-productivity (World Bank, 2020, pp. 1, 5–7, 32–33). The matched indicators therefore support the phrase **“structural change without comparable manufacturing deepening,”** not “no transformation.”

Korea’s trajectory combined rising manufacturing weight, export upgrading, and much earlier movement out of agriculture. Its 1960 commodity exports were 86.3% primary products and 13.7% manufactures in Kim’s historical classification; that is valid evidence for Korea in 1960, not a category that can be connected to a modern six-country export chart (Kim, 1971, Table 1, pp. 16–17). Korea’s policy sequence included land reform and reconstruction, export-oriented incentives, state-influenced credit linked to export performance, and later heavy and chemical industry promotion; debt and capacity pressures then prompted adjustment and liberalisation rather than an uninterrupted master plan (World Bank, 1993, pp. 127–130, 159–161).

### 2.3 What the pair cannot prove

Two countries cannot identify a continental cause. Similar 1960 GDP per capita does not mean identical starting conditions, and endpoint correlations do not isolate education, policy, institutions, geography, aid, war, or culture. Korea’s 71% adult-literacy estimate for 1960 is informative but cannot be turned into a Ghana–Korea ratio because Ghana has no approved matched value; the estimate itself is for people aged 15+ and may fall within two years of the label (World Bank, 1980, Table 23, printed p. 155/PDF p. 165; definition printed p. 158/PDF p. 168; timing note printed p. 164/PDF p. 174). The pair also cannot show that manufacturing is the only route to higher income.

### The live scholarly disagreement

The World Bank’s comparative account credits sound macroeconomics, human capital, high saving and investment, agricultural productivity, infrastructure, and openness to technology, while allowing a conditional role for selective interventions disciplined by performance and capable institutions. It also says the available evidence cannot construct clean counterfactuals and that there was no single East Asian model (World Bank, 1993, pp. 4–7, 10–12, 20–26). Rodrik challenges the standard export-led reading of Korea and Taiwan, proposing investment coordination and educated labour relative to capital as a better account of the initial acceleration (Rodrik, 1995, abstract). The magazine should stage this disagreement explicitly: **exports may have disciplined and revealed capability, while coordinated investment helped create the capability that could export.** That is a sequencing question, not a choice between slogans.

## 4. Variation is part of the explanation

### 4.1 Within Africa: Ghana, Botswana, and Mauritius

| Case | Verified pathway | What the contrast demonstrates | What it cannot demonstrate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ghana | Large post-1991 movement out of agriculture, but MVA stayed near 11% of GDP in 2020 and transformation tilted toward services, construction, and mining (World Bank, 2020, pp. 1, 5–7, 32–33; World Bank, 2026a). | Structural change can occur without Korea-like manufacturing deepening. | That services are uniformly unproductive, culture caused the pattern, or industrialisation was impossible. |
| Botswana | GDP per capita rose from $393.86 in 1960 to $6,254.25 in 2020, while MVA was only 5.66% in 2020; diamonds were 78.8% of the 1990 export total under the IMF table and 88.2% under the 2020 national principal-group table (International Monetary Fund, 1998, Table 4; Statistics Botswana, 2021, Table 2.2, p. 14; World Bank, 2026a). | High income growth can arise from resource rents and institutions without broad manufacturing diversification; “growth” and “structural transformation” are not synonyms. | A comparable diamond trend: the 1990 and 2020 categories and re-export treatments differ. Nor does it prove resources inevitably block diversification. |
| Mauritius | Sugar supplied more than 95% of export earnings near independence; by 1996 its export share had fallen to 24%, EPZ employment had reached about 80,000, and tourism had expanded (National Archives Department, n.d., “The Economy”; World Bank, 1997, Executive Summary, pp. 1–2). In matched WDI data, agricultural employment fell from 19.6% in 1991 to 5.3% in 2020 (World Bank, 2026a). | An African economy diversified through sugar rents/preferences, export manufacturing, tourism, institutions, and networks. It directly rejects “Africa could not industrialise or diversify.” | That the island model scales to larger states, that every policy caused the result, or that merchandise data capture tourism and finance. |

**Within-Africa conclusion:** these cases share neither one outcome nor one mechanism. The useful comparison is institutional and structural: how commodity or preference rents were converted—or not converted—into broader capabilities. It is not a cultural ranking (Hillbom, 2014, pp. 155–176; Svirydzenka & Petri, 2014, pp. 5–8, 12).

### 4.2 Within Asia: South Korea, Malaysia, and the Philippines

| Case | Verified pathway | What the contrast demonstrates | What it cannot demonstrate |
|---|---|---|---|
| South Korea | Domestic capability building, export-linked discipline, state-coordinated investment, land reform, and later adjustment; MVA reached 25.24% in 1990 and 25.70% in 2020 (World Bank, 1993, pp. 127–130, 159–161; World Bank, 2026a). | A deep industrial route with strong domestic coordination and learning. | That all Korean interventions worked, that policy alone caused growth, or that the sequence is directly portable. |
| Malaysia | Commodity inheritance followed by EPZ/FDI-led electronics; MVA rose from 10.26% in 1960 to 24.22% in 1990, peaked at 30.94% in 1999, and was 22.23% in 2020 (World Bank, 2020, pp. 44–47; World Bank, 2026a). | Asian industrialisation included a multinational-led route distinct from Korea’s. | That electronics exports equal Malaysian value added or technological ownership; later productivity and skills constraints remained (World Bank, 2016, pp. 28–50). |

**Within-Asia conclusion:** “export-oriented Asia” contained at least three distinct production systems. Korea, Malaysia, and the Philippines differ in domestic supplier depth, state coordination, multinational dependence, services, and policy sequence. Shared geography or broad cultural labels do not explain those differences.

## 5. Manufactured exports versus commodities: usable evidence and a hard boundary

### 5.1 Narrative evidence that survives review

- **Ghana:** a government plan reported cocoa and cocoa products at roughly 60% of export income using 1959–1962 evidence. This supports historical commodity concentration, not an exact 1960 product-share bar (Government of Ghana, 1964, PDF p. 224).
- **South Korea:** the 1960 historical table reports primary products at 86.3% and manufactures at 13.7% of commodity exports. Modern product identities include integrated circuits and cars, but no approved common taxonomy connects those endpoints (Kim, 1971, Table 1, pp. 16–17; World Bank, n.d., 2020 trade-summary query).
- **Botswana:** the IMF’s 1990 table implies diamonds at 78.8% of its listed total; Statistics Botswana reports 88.2% in 2020. Keep both figures in prose with separate definitions; do not draw a connected line (International Monetary Fund, 1998, Table 4; Statistics Botswana, 2021, Table 2.2, p. 14).
- **Mauritius:** the defensible story is movement from extreme sugar dependence toward EPZ manufacturing, tourism, and finance. Merchandise-only comparisons omit the service side of the transformation (National Archives Department, n.d., “The Economy”; World Bank, 1997, Executive Summary, pp. 1–2).
- **Malaysia:** source-specific evidence supports a move from rubber and tin toward manufactures and electronics, but the historical authority categories and 2020 SITC product groups are not one series; the registered legacy tables also require publisher/archival reopening before publication of exact shares (International Monetary Fund, 1999, Table IV.3; Department of Statistics Malaysia, 2021, Table 2, pp. 4–5).
- **Philippines:** source-specific tables support a shift from primary exports toward electronics and other manufactures, with an important imported-input qualification. Historical and 2020 national groupings differ (Nasution, 2000, pp. 19–20 and Table 9, p. 20; Philippine Statistics Authority, 2021, Figure 3 and Table 2).

### 5.2 The blocked visual

**Do not create:** six-country manufactured-versus-commodity bars for 1960/1990/2020; connected export-share lines; a “commodity to manufacturing” slopegraph; or any redrawn series that treats the six national/source-specific classifications as equivalent.

The blocked series would mix historical authority categories, SITC and HS groupings, merchandise and “export income,” half-year and full-year denominators, services omissions, and uncertain re-export treatment. It would look comparable while changing the meaning of the numerator and denominator. The Philippines historical MVA observations are also from incompatible national-account vintages and must not be spliced into the current WDI series (*Six-country comparability review*, 2026, pp. 4–5, 9–10).

**Revision condition:** unlock the export chart only after saving one UN Comtrade/WITS extraction for every country and anchor year with reporter, year, export flow, world partner, one classification/revision, included codes, aggregation map, FOB denominator, returned values, retrieval date, and a documented rule for re-exports.

## 7. Approved claims

1. **Structural transformation has at least two parts:** productivity changes within sectors and labour moving between sectors; employment movement alone is not sufficient evidence of successful transformation (McMillan et al., 2014, p. 11).
2. **Ghana experienced major labour reallocation after 1991 but not Korea-like manufacturing deepening.** This is supported by matched employment, productivity, MVA, and GDP indicators plus the Ghana structural-transformation diagnostic (World Bank, 2020, pp. 1, 5–7, 32–33; World Bank, 2026a).
3. **Korea’s industrialisation was sequenced and multi-causal.** Capability building, education, land/agricultural change, infrastructure, investment coordination, export-linked discipline, technology acquisition, and international conditions interacted; policy mistakes and later corrections matter (World Bank, 1993, pp. 20–26, 79–80, 127–130, 159–161).
4. **The role of exports is disputed.** One strong account stresses performance discipline and access to technology; another argues that coordinated investment better explains the initial acceleration (Rodrik, 1995, abstract; World Bank, 1993, pp. 21–24).
5. **Africa and Asia each contain meaningful pathway variation.** Mauritius and Botswana challenge a single African story; Malaysia and the Philippines challenge a single East Asian formula (Hillbom, 2014, pp. 155–176; Svirydzenka & Petri, 2014, pp. 5–8; World Bank, 2020, pp. 44–47).
6. **Manufactured exports are neither identical to manufacturing value added nor proof of domestic technological depth.** Gross trade can include imported inputs and foreign-owned assembly (Nasution, 2000, pp. 19–20; World Bank, 2020, p. 46).
7. **The evidence rejects cultural determinism.** Cultural norms may shape coordination or legitimacy in particular settings, but the verified economic record requires institutions, incentives, capabilities, resources, history, and international conditions to remain in the explanation.

## 8. Unresolved questions

- How much of each country’s productivity change came from within-sector gains versus labour reallocation? A consistent six-country shift-share decomposition has not been approved.
- How much domestic value was retained in Malaysian and Philippine electronics exports, and how did local supplier depth change over time?
- Which Ghana service subsectors absorbed labour, and how did their productivity and job quality compare with manufacturing?
- How did distributional outcomes affect the durability and legitimacy of each industrial-policy settlement?
- Can comparable firm-level learning, export survival, or supplier-linkage evidence be obtained for all six cases without expanding beyond the magazine’s smallest sufficient evidence set?
- What comparable education-quality or technical-skill measures exist near each country’s take-off? Adult literacy is too coarse and incomplete.

## 9. Rejected or overstated claims

- “Africa could not grow like Asia because of culture.” The cases and mechanisms do not support either continental uniformity or cultural determinism.
- “Ghana did not structurally transform.” It did; the more precise claim is that reallocation did not produce Korea-like manufacturing deepening.
- “Korea grew because it exported.” Export discipline is plausible, but investment coordination, skills, land/agricultural change, technology, macro stability, institutions, and international conditions are competing or complementary explanations.
- “Industrial policy caused the East Asian miracle.” Some interventions helped under particular administrative and disciplinary conditions; sector targeting also produced costs and failures.
- “Manufactured exports prove domestic industrial capability.” They can include imported inputs, foreign ownership, and assembly with limited local value capture.
- “Botswana disproves the importance of manufacturing.” Botswana shows that resource-led income growth is possible; it also illustrates diversification limits.
- “Mauritius is proof that any African country can copy an island export-zone model.” Scale, preferences, diaspora networks, geography, and services make direct transplantation unsafe.
- “Education was the strongest explanatory variable.” No approved matched six-country historical literacy panel or causal estimate supports that ranking.

2. **Structural-transformation anatomy:** a compact diagram separating movement of workers, within-sector productivity, manufacturing weight, exports, and income.
3. **Africa pathways triptych:** Ghana, Botswana, Mauritius with matched employment and income/MVA outcomes plus non-comparable export annotations.
4. **East Asian mechanism routes:** Korea, Malaysia, Philippines shown as three distinct sequences with shared nodes but different ownership, policy, and learning patterns.
5. **Export debate card:** “exports as performance test” versus “investment as the initial engine,” with source locators and a question prompt.
6. **Korea policy timeline with reversals:** include HCI bottlenecks and adjustment so the visual does not become a triumphalist arrow.

No export-composition visual is approved for cross-country or time-series comparison.

## 12. Recommendation for THEORY-001

Adopt a **conditional capabilities-and-coordination thesis**:

> Divergence depended less on fixed regional culture than on whether states, firms, workers, and external partners built and disciplined productive capabilities over time. Education, land and agricultural change, infrastructure, investment coordination, technology access, and exports mattered in different sequences; their effects depended on institutional capacity, distribution, resources, and international conditions. Culture should enter only through specific, evidenced mechanisms—such as legitimacy, coordination, or organisational practice—and must compete with non-cultural explanations.

THEORY-001 should structure the matrix around five tests:

1. **Mechanism:** what behaviour or institutional process links the proposed factor to productivity?
2. **Sequence:** did the factor precede take-off, emerge with it, or follow it?
3. **Discipline and learning:** what measured results determined whether support continued?
4. **Countercase:** which locked country most strongly challenges the claim?
5. **Revision condition:** what evidence would narrow or overturn it?

Treat Hofstede-style national scores as hypotheses about possible coordination patterns, not causal inputs. Require every cultural claim to explain Ghana–Mauritius/Botswana and Korea–Malaysia/Philippines variation at least as well as the institutional and economic alternatives.
