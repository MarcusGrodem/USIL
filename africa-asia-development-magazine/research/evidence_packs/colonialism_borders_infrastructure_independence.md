## How to read this pack

- **[F] Directly documented historical fact:** a dated primary/official record, archive catalogue, historical dataset, or a secondary work documenting a bounded event or condition.
- **[I] Scholarly interpretation:** an author's explanation of a pattern or mechanism.
- **[C] Competing explanation or qualification:** evidence that narrows a broad causal claim.
- **[G] Group-theory implication:** a question for the original Connected Development Theory. This is not established scholarship and must be labelled “our theory” in the magazine.
- “Approved” means supportable at the cited scope and wording. It does not mean the claim is causal beyond the design of the cited research.

## H1 — Colonial control was layered, sequential, and interrupted

**Reader question:** Who ruled the six cases, and when did control change?

### Evidence

- **[F] Ghana:** The Gold Coast entered independence in 1957 after British rule. A World Bank retrospective describes the independence economy as cocoa-dependent but also records reserves, basic services, roads, a functioning railway, technical education, and Tema harbour under construction (World Bank, 1985, printed p. 18, PDF p. 27, paras. 1.01–1.03).
- **[F] Botswana:** Bechuanaland was a British protectorate governed largely through chiefs and became independent on 30 September 1966 (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–4).
- **[F] Mauritius:** French rule was followed by British rule; slavery was followed by indentured labour, principally from India, in the plantation economy (Government of Mauritius, n.d., “History” section; UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2006, printed pp. 22–25). Mauritius became independent on 12 March 1968 (National Archives Department, n.d., “Pre-independence Mauritius” and “The Economy” sections).
- **[F] Korea:** Japan annexed Korea in 1910 and colonial rule ended in 1945; the subsequent territorial division and Korean War broke any simple line from colonial plant or bureaucracy to the later Republic of Korea (Haggard et al., 1997, pp. 867–870, 875–877).
- **[F] Malaysia:** The present country cannot be treated as a unitary 1957 colony: Malaya's colonial commodity and labour structure preceded the later federation, and Sabah and Sarawak had distinct territorial histories. Any 1960 “Malaysia” comparison is therefore retrospective rather than a contemporaneous sovereign-country observation (Asadullah et al., 2024, pp. 13–15; World Bank, 2026, Malaysia country metadata and series notes).
- **[F] Philippines:** Spanish rule formed a church-linked landed order and incorporated local elites; U.S. rule subsequently expanded representative institutions, the civil service, education, and communications while also incorporating established elites. Japanese wartime occupation interrupted U.S. rule before 1946 independence (Dolan, 1993, pp. 5–6, 28–34, 76–77, 108–109, 146–147).

### What this supports, challenges, and cannot prove

- **Supports:** “Colonial inheritance” is a set of dated layers, not a binary colonized/not-colonized variable.
- **[C] Challenges:** A single 1914 control map cannot represent Korea after 1945, the Philippines' sequential rulers, Mauritius's French-to-British transition, or the later formation of Malaysia.
- **Alternative explanations:** war, partition, post-independence state formation, commodity discoveries, external markets, and domestic policy choices changed the inherited starting point.
- **Cannot prove:** that the identity of the colonizer, by itself, caused any 1990 or 2020 development outcome.

## H2 — British rule varied across places and within colonies

**Reader question:** Did British rule leave the same inheritance everywhere?

### Evidence

| Case | Documented inheritance | Qualification or counterexample |
|---|---|---|
| Ghana | **[F]** Export-oriented transport and cocoa dependence coexisted with roads, feeder roads, a railway reported in good condition, harbour investment, education, public services, and reserves at independence (World Bank, 1985, printed p. 18, PDF p. 27, paras. 1.01–1.03). | **[C]** This directly counters “colonial rule left nothing,” while the same source counters “infrastructure was neutral”: it says the system was oriented toward import–export trade (World Bank, 1985, printed p. 18, PDF p. 27, para. 1.03). |
| Botswana | **[F]** The protectorate had little investment beyond administration and the railway; at independence more than 90% depended on subsistence agriculture, there were only 22 university graduates, and eight kilometres of tarred road (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–4). | **[C]** British rule did not produce Ghana's material inheritance here. Preserved consultative institutions and deep infrastructure scarcity existed together (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–5; Hillbom, 2014, pp. 155–176). |
| Mauritius | **[F]** British government followed French settlement and plantation institutions; indentured migration after slavery reshaped labour and demography. Near independence, sugar and by-products supplied about 95% of export revenue (UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2006, printed pp. 22–25; Xenos, 1970, p. 1). | **[C]** Sequential French and British layers make a single “British legacy” label misleading. Plantation concentration was a liability, but accumulated sugar rents, skills, capital, and external preferences later became inputs to diversification (Svirydzenka & Petri, 2014, pp. 5–8). |
| Malaya/Malaysia | **[F]** Colonial production and migration produced sectoral and ethnic segmentation that remained relevant to later distributional policy; the later federation joined territories with different histories (Asadullah et al., 2024, pp. 13–15). | **[C]** Malaysia's later manufacturing strategy and infrastructure coordination were policy changes, not automatic colonial continuities (World Bank, 2020, Box 2, pp. 44–47). |

### Cross-colony interpretation

- **[I]** The latest systematic study of more than 450 subnational units finds that British indirect rule was not a uniform system of unconstrained solo chiefs: councils were widespread, often linked to precolonial arrangements, and colonial reforms sometimes restored constraints after installed agents proved ineffective (Bolt et al., 2025, abstract, pp. 1–6, 18–20, and Gold Coast case, pp. 28–31).
- **[C]** The dataset reports a solo chief in only 17% of coded colonial units; that result rejects a universal institutional template, not the existence of coercive colonial power (Bolt et al., 2025, Table 1 and discussion, pp. 18–20).
- **Cannot prove:** that surviving councils were democratic, inclusive, or sufficient for growth; nor that later outcomes were caused by the council form.

## H3 — French/British contrasts are real but not a two-box law

**Reader question:** Can “British indirect/French direct” explain colonial variation?

### Evidence

- **[F]** Archival fiscal data for nine French and four British West African colonies show major commonalities: spending per capita rose sharply in the final colonial decades, and both empires relied heavily on customs revenue and later metropolitan subsidies (Cogneau et al., 2018, abstract and pp. 1–4, 17–20).
- **[I]** The same comparison finds education differences, including a larger education expenditure share in the British sample by 1955, but it concludes that overall public-finance patterns were more similar than a simple empire contrast suggests (Cogneau et al., 2018, pp. 5–7, 19–20, Figures 6–7).
- **[F]** “Direct” French rule still used African authorities, while “indirect” British administrations sometimes invented or reorganized chieftaincy; forced labour, missions, and education differed in degree and form (Cogneau et al., 2018, pp. 5–7).
- **[I]** Within French West Africa, unequal district-level investments in education, health, and infrastructure predicted unequal later public investment and outcomes. Variation within one empire therefore matters (Huillery, 2009, pp. 176–180, 184–187, 203–208, Figure 8).

### Exception and limits

- **[C] Meaningful exception:** Huillery's district variation and Cogneau et al.'s cross-empire similarities both contradict the broad claim that every French colony received one uniform “direct-rule inheritance.”
- **Supports:** compare specific mechanisms—taxation, schooling, labour coercion, councils, and investment—not just imperial flags.
- **Challenges:** “British equals indirect and growth-friendly; French equals direct and growth-hostile.”
- **Alternative explanations:** precolonial institutions, settler presence, ecology, commodity opportunities, metropolitan policy changes, and the timing of late-colonial spending.
- **Cannot prove:** that one empire's average caused the six countries' later income ranking. None of the locked African cases was a French colony at independence; French evidence is therefore a comparative check, not a substitute country case.

## H4 — Other colonial models and major discontinuities

**Reader question:** What changes when the colonial model was Japanese, Spanish–American, or sequential French–British?

### Korea: developmental inheritance versus rupture

- **[I] Interpretation A:** Kohli argues that Japanese colonial rule built a coercive, production-oriented state and industrial structures that formed part of the lineage of South Korea's later developmental state (Kohli, 1994, pp. 1269–1271, 1277–1284, 1288–1291).
- **[C] Interpretation B:** Haggard, Kang, and Moon argue that colonial growth was modest, ownership and benefits were highly unequal, much heavy industry lay in the north, and division and war destroyed or displaced assets; they give greater causal weight to post-1961 policy and politics (Haggard et al., 1997, pp. 867–870, 872–881).
- **Approved synthesis:** Korea inherited some plant, skills, and administrative capacities, but coercion, Japanese ownership, north–south industrial geography, division, war, land reform, aid, and post-1961 policy prevent a straight colonial-to-miracle narrative.
- **Cannot prove:** that colonial coercion was necessary for later growth, or that the later state simply continued the colonial one.

### Philippines: institutional expansion with elite continuity

- **[F]** Spanish institutions linked church, land, and local elites; U.S. rule expanded formal representative institutions, public administration, education, and communications while incorporating established landed elites (Dolan, 1993, pp. 5–6, 28–31, 76–77, 108–109, 146–147).
- **[I]** Preferential access to the U.S. market and later import-substitution policy helped shape industrial incentives, so the inheritance combined institutional assets with external dependence and concentrated power (World Bank, 1987, Vol. II, pp. 1–10).
- **[C] Meaningful exception:** Institutional expansion did not automatically disperse land or political power; formal institutions and elite continuity coexisted.
- **Cannot prove:** that education or American institutions alone explain later English-language services, or that present elite structures are unchanged colonial remnants.

### Mauritius: sequential layers, then adaptation

- **[F]** French plantation/slavery structures and British indenture existed in sequence, not as mutually exclusive national models (Government of Mauritius, n.d., “History” section; UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2006, printed pp. 22–25).
- **[I]** Post-independence diversification reused and altered inherited sugar rents, skills, preferences, and commercial networks through EPZ, tourism, and financial policies (Svirydzenka & Petri, 2014, pp. 5–8).
- **[C] Meaningful exception:** a concentrated plantation inheritance supplied both a serious dependency and some resources later repurposed; “liability” and “asset” are not always different objects.

## H5 — Infrastructure had builders, owners, purposes, and afterlives

**Reader question:** Who built the routes, what were they for, and what happened to them later?

### Ghana railway evidence ledger

| Dimension | Supportable statement | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | **[F]** The principal colonial lines were built from 1898 to 1918, connecting the coast with gold-mining areas and Kumasi; route sequence and openings must be shown by segment, not as one instantaneous network. | Jedwab & Moradi (2012), pp. 1–2, 6–10, Figures 1–3 |
| Ownership/authority | **[F]** The system was a Gold Coast colonial-government investment. Caption it “colonial government railway,” not simply “British railway,” and verify segment-level operators before naming an owner. | Jedwab & Moradi (2012), pp. 6–10 |
| Route purpose | **[F/I]** Mining, administrative connection, and access to Kumasi were important; the network also lowered transport costs and enabled cocoa expansion. Purpose at authorization and later economic use must be separate labels. | Jedwab & Moradi (2012), pp. 1–4, 6–10, Figures 1–3 |
| Later use/effect | **[I]** The authors link railway access to cocoa production, urbanization, and persistent spatial change even after rail's later relative decline. This is an econometric interpretation, not proof that every rail line caused broad welfare gains. | Jedwab & Moradi (2012), abstract, pp. 1–4 |
| Independence condition | **[F]** The railway was reported in good condition at independence, within a transport system explicitly described as import–export oriented. | World Bank (1985), printed p. 18, PDF p. 27, para. 1.03 |

### Comparative qualification

- **[C] Meaningful exception to “all railways were mine-to-port extraction”:** Ghana's system served mining and colonial trade but also Kumasi and a new cocoa frontier; construction motive, ownership, route purpose, and later use changed over time (Jedwab & Moradi, 2012, pp. 1–4, 6–10).
- **[C] Meaningful exception to “railways were development gifts”:** transport gains coexisted with a network oriented to overseas trade rather than balanced national integration (World Bank, 1985, printed p. 18, PDF p. 27, para. 1.03).
- **Locked-case contrasts:** Botswana inherited very little road infrastructure despite a railway (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–4); Korea's inherited industry and transport were geographically disrupted by division and war (Haggard et al., 1997, pp. 872–877); Malaysia's later electronics corridors depended on postcolonial policy coordination rather than track inheritance alone (World Bank, 2020, Box 2, pp. 44–47).
- **Alternative explanations:** crop suitability, market access, mineral location, urban demand, later roads and ports, war destruction, and post-independence maintenance.
- **Cannot prove:** that colonial infrastructure was nationally optimal, evenly accessible, intended to raise local welfare, or sufficient for later industrialization.

## H6 — Borders partitioned peoples, but not every line ignored prior geography

**Reader question:** What did a partitioned border split—and what did it follow?

### Evidence

- **[I]** Across Africa, partitioned ethnic homelands are associated with more conflict, discrimination, and weaker public-good provision in the authors' border-based research design (Michalopoulos & Papaioannou, 2016, pp. 1802–1813, 1821–1838, Figure 1 and Tables 3–8).
- **[F/I]** A newer reconstruction finds that historical political frontiers directly affected 62% of 107 bilateral borders and that major water bodies also mattered. Borders could therefore be coercive and partitioning without being universally random straight lines (Paine et al., 2025, pp. 1–3, 6–12, Table 1).
- **[F] Ghana–Togo detail:** After World War I, the powers revised the former Gold Coast–German Togoland boundary so that Dagomba was reunited within British Togoland/Gold Coast, while discussion of an Ewe revision did not reunite the more decentralized Ewe cultural area (Paine et al., 2025, pp. 13–14, Figure 7d and Supplementary Appendix C.3.4).
- **[C] Meaningful exception:** The Dagomba revision challenges “Europeans ignored every African frontier”; the Ewe partition on the same boundary challenges any claim that historical or cultural geography was consistently respected (Paine et al., 2025, pp. 13–14, Figure 7d).

### Interpretation limits

- **Supports:** partition is a plausible institutional and political mechanism worth testing.
- **Challenges:** “Africa's borders were drawn at one meeting in Berlin,” “every line was arbitrary,” and “straightness proves arbitrariness.”
- **Alternative explanations:** precolonial political geography, rivers and lakes, bargaining among European powers, African treaties and lobbying, colonial administrative revisions, and post-independence policy.
- **Cannot prove:** that borders alone caused Ghana's, Botswana's, or Mauritius's later economic path; that every person within an ethnographic polygon shared one political identity; or that moving borders now would improve welfare.

### Proposed visual H6-MAP — one boundary, two outcomes

- **Date represented:** boundary process after Germany's World War I defeat, with the relevant revision labelled **1919/postwar settlement**; avoid presenting the current Ghana–Togo boundary as if it were fixed in 1884.
- **Geographic boundaries:** the former Gold Coast–German Togoland/British Togoland segment, precolonial Dagomba frontier, and generalized Ewe cultural-area polygon; no unsupported continent-wide ethnic mosaic.
- **Source:** Paine et al. (2025), Figure 7d, pp. 13–14, Supplementary Appendix C.3.4; replication dataset (Paine et al., 2024), files underlying Figure 7d.
- **Transformation required:** reproduce only with permission/licence confirmation or redraw from licensed replication layers; distinguish colonial boundary proposals, final/postwar boundary, political-state frontier, and generalized cultural-area polygon through line pattern as well as colour.
- **Known uncertainty:** the Ewe layer derives from Murdock's mid-twentieth-century ethnographic reconstruction and must not imply timeless, exact, or exclusive identity. Colonial treaties and maps record negotiated claims, not complete administrative control. Verify replication-file licences and exact boundary dates before artwork.

**Reader question:** What usable assets and binding liabilities existed at political independence?

|---|---|---|---|

The inventory does not identify its later net effect. |
| Botswana, 1966 | Chiefly/consultative structures and a railway existed; post-independence authorities could build on some institutional continuity (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–5; Hillbom, 2014, pp. 155–176). | More than 90% in subsistence agriculture, 22 graduates, eight kilometres of tarred road, and little colonial investment (Maipose, 2008, pp. 3–4). | Later diamonds and policy choices are not colonial assets; discovery and mineral-rights decisions belong after independence (Maipose, 2008, pp. 5, 10–11). |

| South Korea, 1945/1948 state formation | Some industrial plant, skills, transport, and administrative capacity existed (Kohli, 1994, pp. 1277–1284). | Coercion and unequal ownership, northern concentration of heavy industry, territorial division, and war sharply limited continuity (Haggard et al., 1997, pp. 867–877). | Use a two-stage label: end of Japanese rule (1945) and Republic of Korea state formation (1948), followed by war. “At independence” is not one clean asset snapshot. |

Do not assign Sabah and Sarawak an undifferentiated “Malayan” inheritance. |

They do not predict one development path. |

### Group-theory implication, explicitly not an established finding

- **[G] Connected Development Theory question:** Did inherited networks connect producers, workers, knowledge, and domestic regions broadly enough to support later coordination, or mostly connect enclaves to external markets?
- The historical evidence makes this testable, but it does **not** validate the theory. A fair test must compare inherited network shape with post-independence expansion, maintenance, access, and policy adaptation.
- Botswana and South Korea are critical challenges: weak inherited infrastructure did not prevent Botswana's later growth, while Korea's material inheritance was heavily interrupted. Mauritius is a second challenge because a narrow export system was partly repurposed.

## Approved-claim list

Use only at the stated scope and retain the indicated citations.

1. **A1:** The six cases experienced different colonial sequences and different transitions; “colonial inheritance” is not one event (sources and locators in H1).
2. **A2:** Ghana inherited both export dependence and usable public/transport assets; either half alone is misleading (World Bank, 1985, printed p. 18/PDF p. 27, paras. 1.01–1.03).
3. **A3:** British rule varied substantially across Ghana, Botswana, Mauritius, and Malaya/Malaysia in administrative form, investment, and inherited capacity (H2 country locators; Bolt et al., 2025, pp. 1–6, 18–20, 28–31).
4. **A4:** British “indirect rule” was heterogeneous and often included councils and locally specific reforms (Bolt et al., 2025, abstract, pp. 1–6, 18–20).
5. **A5:** British and French West African colonial finances differed in some domains, including education, but shared important revenue and expenditure patterns (Cogneau et al., 2018, pp. 5–7, 17–20, Figures 6–7).
6. **A6:** French colonial investment varied sharply within French West Africa and had persistent local associations/effects in Huillery's research design (Huillery, 2009, pp. 176–180, 184–187, 203–208, Figure 8).
7. **A7:** Korea's colonial inheritance is contested: some scholarship stresses state/industrial lineage, while competing work stresses unequal ownership, division, war, and post-1961 change (Kohli, 1994, pp. 1269–1291; Haggard et al., 1997, pp. 867–881).
8. **A8:** In the Philippines, expanded formal institutions and education coexisted with elite and land concentration (Dolan, 1993, pp. 5–6, 28–31, 76–77, 108–109, 146–147).
9. **A9:** Ghana's colonial railway had multiple initial and later functions; construction, authority, route purpose, and later use must be distinguished (Jedwab & Moradi, 2012, pp. 1–4, 6–10, Figures 1–3).
10. **A10:** Partitioned ethnic homelands can matter for conflict and public goods, but not all African borders ignored historical frontiers or geography (Michalopoulos & Papaioannou, 2016, pp. 1802–1838; Paine et al., 2025, pp. 1–14, Table 1 and Figure 7).
11. **A11:** The Ghana–Togo boundary detail contains both a historical-frontier accommodation (Dagomba) and a partitioned cultural area (Ewe), making it a useful counterexample within one visual (Paine et al., 2025, pp. 13–14, Figure 7d).
12. **A12:** At independence, assets and liabilities frequently came from the same system—rail connected and channelled, sugar financed and concentrated, formal institutions administered and excluded (H7 locators). This is a comparative synthesis, not a standalone causal estimate.
