# Original Group Theories

These are working theories developed for the project after the teacher asked the group to create its own theories. They are not established academic theories and must not be presented as such.

The theories should guide questions and case selection. They should not take over the entire magazine.

## Theory standard

Each theory needs:

- a clear claim;
- a mechanism explaining how it could work;
- a prediction;
- evidence that would support it;
- evidence that would challenge it;
- limits and alternative explanations;
- a record of how the theory changed during research.

## Theory 1: Connected Development Theory

### Reader question

> What if the decisive difference was not the amount of infrastructure, but whom it connected?

### Working claim

Economic development is more likely to spread when transport, energy, institutions and markets connect domestic firms, workers, cities and regional supply chains. Infrastructure designed mainly to move raw materials from a mine or plantation to a port may increase exports without producing the same domestic industrial network.

### Proposed mechanism

1. Connections reduce the cost of moving goods, people, information and power.
2. Firms gain access to suppliers, workers and customers.
3. Larger connected markets make investment and specialisation more attractive.
4. Firms learn from one another and enter more complex value chains.
5. Productivity and diversification spread beyond an export enclave.

### Predictions

- Countries with stronger internal and regional connections should diversify faster, all else equal.
- Export corridors that remain isolated from domestic markets should have weaker spillovers.
- Industrial clusters should be more likely where power, ports, roads, skills and suppliers connect.
- Regional market integration may partly compensate for small national markets.

### Evidence needed

- transport and electricity maps;
- freight or logistics costs;
- domestic and regional trade data;
- manufacturing location and cluster evidence;
- export-enclave case studies;
- time-series evidence before and after major network improvements.

### Possible supporting cases

- East Asian production and export networks;
- industrial corridors that connected suppliers and ports;
- African regional or national cases with expanding industrial clusters.

### What would challenge the theory

- a country industrialising rapidly despite persistent domestic disconnection;
- a highly connected economy failing to diversify for reasons the theory cannot explain;
- evidence that connections followed rather than caused industrial growth.

### Limitations

- Connection may be necessary but not sufficient.
- Political stability, skills, trade access, institutions and finance may determine whether networks are used productively.
- Poorly planned infrastructure can create debt without capability.
- Digital and institutional connection may matter even when physical infrastructure is weak.

### Current status

Working hypothesis. Not yet tested.

## Theory 2: Radius of Trust Theory

### Reader question

> How far beyond family and close networks can economic trust travel?

### Working claim

Firms and formal markets may scale more easily when people can trust contracts, courts, banks, professional managers and unfamiliar partners. When trust is concentrated inside family or close networks, those networks can support survival and entrepreneurship but may make delegation and large impersonal organisations harder.

### Proposed mechanism

1. Trust reduces the perceived risk of dealing with strangers.
2. Owners are more willing to delegate money and decisions.
3. Firms can hire on skill rather than only relationship.
4. Credit, contracts and supply relationships can extend farther.
5. Formal organisations grow beyond a close personal network.

### Predictions

- Broader institutional trust should be associated with more delegation and larger formal firms.
- Reliable courts and contracts may extend the radius of trust even when interpersonal trust is limited.
- Where formal institutions are weak, firms may rely more heavily on family, ethnic or personal networks.
- Historical shocks that reduced trust may have long economic effects unless institutions provide substitutes.

### Evidence needed

- credible trust surveys;
- institutional-confidence measures;
- firm-size and ownership data;
- management and delegation research;
- contract enforcement and court performance;
- historical studies on trust formation;
- evidence on successful substitutes such as family conglomerates or state-backed firms.

### Possible supporting evidence

- Nunn and Wantchekon research on historical shocks and trust;
- evidence on family firms, professional management and contract enforcement;
- class survey used only as a discussion prompt, not national evidence.

### What would challenge the theory

- low-trust settings repeatedly scaling large firms without institutional or organisational substitutes;
- high-trust settings failing to develop firms for reasons the theory ignores;
- evidence that firm growth causes trust rather than the other way around.

### Limitations

- Trust is difficult to measure and may vary by context.
- Family and community networks can be productive sources of credit, labour and resilience.
- Formal enforcement may substitute for trust.
- Large firms can also grow through state ownership, foreign ownership or concentrated family control.
- Historical evidence does not justify labelling a contemporary culture as deficient.

### Current status

Working hypothesis. Not yet tested.

## Theory 3: Continuity + Adaptation Theory

### Reader question

> Can a country learn industrially if its direction changes every few years?

### Working claim

Industrial capability may require a policy direction that lasts long enough for firms, workers and institutions to learn. Continuity must be combined with feedback and adaptation; otherwise the state may protect failure for decades.

### Proposed mechanism

1. Firms invest when they can anticipate infrastructure, education and trade policy.
2. Workers and schools build skills that match a longer-term direction.
3. Suppliers and financial institutions develop around growing sectors.
4. Repeated production creates learning and quality improvement.
5. Feedback allows failed support to be corrected rather than permanently protected.

### Predictions

- Sustained industrial direction should support capability building and upgrading.
- Frequent policy reversal should weaken investment and learning.
- Continuity without performance discipline should produce protected but uncompetitive sectors.
- Successful cases should show both long-term direction and mechanisms for correction.

### Evidence needed

- policy timelines;
- government changes and policy reversals;
- firm investment and export data;
- industrial-support conditions;
- evidence of learning, productivity and upgrading;
- cases of both successful adaptation and prolonged policy failure.

### Possible supporting cases

- South Korea's sustained export and industrial upgrading direction;
- other East Asian cases with long-term capability building;
- African cases where policy continuity was interrupted or successfully maintained.

### What would challenge the theory

- rapid industrial learning despite frequent major policy reversal;
- stable policy direction with no capability gains, even when other requirements are present;
- evidence that continuity merely reflects prior success rather than causing it.

### Limitations

- Continuity can become rigidity.
- Authoritarian control is not the same as effective continuity.
- External shocks can force policy changes.
- Geopolitics, finance, market access and state capacity may matter more than duration.

### Current status

Working hypothesis. Not yet tested.

## Possible combined theory

The rough storyboard proposes a final synthesis:

> Countries may upgrade faster when people and firms are connected, trust can scale beyond close networks, and policy remains stable long enough to learn while still correcting failure.

This combined statement is not yet the final conclusion. Research may show that one theory is stronger, one needs revision or the three should remain separate.
