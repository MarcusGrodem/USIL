Section 1 · The question Observe · Predict · Test · Revise

Was slower growth caused by culture, or was culture itself shaped by history and institutions?

Topic (assigned)
Economic development in Africa: why this region couldn’t grow like the Asia region — cultural analysis.
The assigned title asserts a conclusion. We treat it as a question. Six locked cases — Ghana, Botswana, Mauritius, South Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines — are observed at three anchor years: 1960, 1990, 2020. Three group hypotheses are then tested against that evidence. Culture may matter, but only through mechanisms the evidence can actually support.

Method · Observe → Predict → Test → Revise

VerbWhat it means here
ObserveRecord what the six economies looked like at 1960, 1990 and 2020 using one harmonised dataset.
PredictWrite what each hypothesis would need to see if it were true.
TestCompare against six cases, not two blocs; register supports and challenges.
ReviseNarrow, broaden, or reject the hypothesis based on what the evidence allows.

Six transitions — different sequences, not one event

What the dates show

The six economies did not start running at the same time. Korea’s 1945 liberation came with a divided peninsula and a war on the horizon; Botswana walked into 1966 with eight kilometres of tarred road.

A date is not an inheritance. Ghana left 1957 with cocoa dependence and usable public and transport assets (World Bank, 1985, Report 5716-GH). The Philippines’ 1946 institutions coexisted with land concentration.

05 · Starting line
Section 2 · Systems of rule Myth · evidence · limit

“Not two boxes.” What different colonial systems actually did.

Scope
Six cases, four empires, inside-empire variation
Imperial flags do not explain institutional variation. The claim that British “indirect rule” was lighter, or that French “direct rule” was more extractive, flattens evidence that was always lumpy. Variation sat inside each empire, not between two neat boxes.
Common claimWhat the evidence showsWhat it cannot be used for
“Indirect rule” was uniform in British Africa. A single chief appears in only 17% of coded British colonial units; councils, elders and multiple chiefs are more common (Bolt, Gardner, Kohler, Paine & Robinson, 2025). Treating “indirect rule” as one institution or inferring Ghana’s or Botswana’s post-1960 trajectory from the label alone.
British and French public finance diverged sharply. Both relied heavily on customs revenue; late-colonial per-capita spending rose in both; education expenditure patterns differed somewhat but not as sharply as narratives claim (Cogneau, Dupraz & Mesplé-Somps, 2018). A single empire-level fiscal story. Nothing causal about post-independence growth paths follows.
French West Africa was uniformly under-invested. District-level investment varied sharply and left persistent local associations with later outcomes (Huillery, 2009). Treating an empire as an undifferentiated whole.
Japanese colonial rule built Korea’s developmental state. Contested. Kohli (1994) stresses institutional continuity; Haggard, Kang & Moon (1997) stress disruption from division, war, land reform and post-1961 change. “Japan created Korea’s miracle.” Independence was not continuous rule.
The Philippines inherited reformable institutions from the US period. Formal institutions expanded alongside elite land concentration (Dolan, 1993, pending clean archival copy). Reading post-1946 outcomes as a straightforward colonial legacy; many later policy choices were decisive.
Mauritius inherited a single colonial system. Successive French and British layers, with indentured labour from India from the 1830s onwards (Mauritius National Archives Department, n.d.; UNESCO WHC, 2006, Aapravasi Ghat). Collapsing Mauritius into either “French” or “British.” Later diversification is multi-source.
Mechanism, not label Colonial systems bequeathed specific assets (railway segments, trained administrators, export markets) and specific liabilities (concentration, extractive revenue mix, settler land holdings). Both often came through the same institution. The question is which combinations later mattered — and under which post-1960 policies.
SourcesBolt et al. (2025) NBER WP 30582; Cogneau, Dupraz & Mesplé-Somps (2018); Huillery (2009) AEJ: Applied Economics, 1(2); Kohli (1994) WD, 22(9); Haggard, Kang & Moon (1997) WD, 25(6); Dolan (1993); Mauritius National Archives (n.d.); UNESCO WHC (2006). RES-001 evidence pack.

Section 2 · Infrastructure Original schematic · Ghana colonial government railway

A railway is four things at once — depending on which layer you look at.

Case
Ghana colonial government railway, 1898–1918
The temptation is to read a line on a map as a benefit. Ghana’s colonial-era railway was built, owned, used and remembered differently depending on the layer. Here we annotate the same original route in four layers at once — not reproducing the copyrighted source map, but rendering an original schematic from the facts Jedwab and Moradi (2012) establish.
Layer 1

Built by / under

Colonial government of the Gold Coast. Construction was state-led, not private-settler. Chinese and local labour were employed on segments.

Layer 2

Owned / administered by

Colonial government department (not a private railway company). Operating authority passed to Ghana at independence; corporate form evolved.

Layer 3

Stated purpose

Open gold-mining districts (Tarkwa, Obuasi); link administration (Kumasi); carry cocoa to coast once production grew. Not a passenger-first system.

Layer 4

Later use / effect

Served cocoa expansion; raised nearby population density; left persistent locational effects (Jedwab & Moradi, 2012) — not a universal welfare uplift.

What this image is An original schematic drawn from stated facts and date-anchored stations. Not a reproduction or tracing of the copyrighted source map. Operator-segment labels are deliberately generic (“colonial government railway”) where the registered evidence does not warrant more.
What it cannot prove Infrastructure proximity is not infrastructure benefit. A line extends to a point; who travels and what travels and whose land it crosses are separate questions. “Afterlife” requires post-1960 investment to persist; many lines did not survive.
SourcesJedwab & Moradi (2012) Colonial investments and long-term development in Africa: Ghanaian railways; World Bank (1985) Report 5716-GH, printed p. 18 / PDF p. 27, paras. 1.01–1.03. 09 · The allocation
Section 3 · The Asian turn Capability loop · not a single Asian model

Capability compounds — when learning is disciplined by markets.

Mechanism
Education → infrastructure → firms → exports → learning → reinvestment → upgrading
Industrial capability in Korea, Malaysia and Mauritius was not one lever but a loop. Each step raises the next: schooling expands the labour pool; infrastructure lowers the cost of production; export-linked credit disciplines firm learning; profits re-enter the loop as upgrading. The loop is not uniquely Asian — but it is uniquely demanding of coordination.
Where this fits Korea (1960–90) built the full loop. Malaysia built most of it with foreign direct investment. Mauritius built the sugar->textile->tourism->finance adaptation of it.
Where it strains Botswana grew rapidly without a manufacturing loop — mineral revenue stewardship took its place. The Philippines produced large electronics export values without comparable firm-level deepening (Nasution, 2000; World Bank, 2020).
Why culture is not sufficient None of the arrows run through “values.” Each depends on investment, policy, markets, discipline, and the international conditions of the day.
SourcesWorld Bank (1993) The East Asian Miracle, pp. 20–26, 79–80, 127–130, 159–161; McMillan, Rodrik & Verduzco (2014) WD, 63, p. 11; Nasution (2000); World Bank (2020). Diagram: original editorial, Claude Opus draft, 2026-10-12.

What exports do not measure

Share-of-exports does not measure value added at home, firm-level learning, or who captures the surplus. Headquarters and ownership matter.

A product journey

An electronics good exported from the Philippines or Malaysia may be designed abroad, assembled with imported inputs, re-exported through regional hubs, and finally invoiced from a third country. The export line is the end of a long chain.

A direct quote worth keeping
By 1992 almost 90% of electronic products in Malaysia were made by transnational-corporation affiliates.

Section 3 · South Korea Four eras · one country · not a “miracle”

South Korea: capability built over time — not by culture alone.

Range
1948 – 2020
Korea’s income rose from US$1,038 in 1960 to US$33,216 in 2020 — a factor of thirty-two. The World Bank’s own analysis (1993) names the mechanisms: capability building, education, land reform, infrastructure, investment coordination, export-linked discipline, technology acquisition, aid, security guarantees, mistakes, and later adjustment. “Confucian culture” explains none of these on its own.
1948 – 1960

Postwar foundations

Land reform (1949–50) redistributes large holdings. US aid pays for food, textbooks, roads. Basic education expands. GDP per capita in 1960: US$1,038.

World Bank (1993) pp. 127–130; Haggard, Kang & Moon (1997).

1960s

Export push & discipline

Credit allocated against export targets; firm-level performance measured; infrastructure extended. Primary exports still dominate (86.3% in 1960; Kim, 1971) but shift.

Kim (1971); World Bank (1993) pp. 20–26.

1970s – 80s

HCI push, debt, adjustment

Heavy & chemical industries subsidised; rural electrification completed by 1979. Mistakes build up; 1980s adjustment prunes overcapacity. 1990 income: US$9,673.

World Bank (1993) pp. 159–161.

1990 – 2020

Upgrading & headwinds

Manufacturing share peaks (29.0% in 2011; still 25.7% in 2020). WGI Rule of Law 1.16 (2020). 2020 income US$33,216 — 16.86× Ghana’s.

14 · Hofstede
Section 4 · Trust Five constructs · not one scale

“Trust” is five different things — and they are not on one scale.

Trap
Collapsing institutions, perceptions and personal ties into one number.
The OECD (2017) explicitly distinguishes five constructs — generalized trust, limited-network trust, named-institution trust, governance (perceived), and survey-response effects. They are measured differently and move for different reasons. Averaging them, or plotting WGI as “trust,” misreads the evidence.
Why WGI ≠ trust Worldwide Governance Indicators are a perception-based governance composite, measured since 1996. The 2020 Rule-of-Law score is not a measure of how much citizens trust their neighbours.
Why six-country trust charts fail No matched 1960/1990 trust data exists for the six cases; the wording of “most people can be trusted” varies; small subsamples (e.g., 64/1,200 in WVS Philippines Wave 7) are not population estimates.
What this does not rule out Institutional substitutes — contracts, courts, business associations — can plausibly extend cooperation beyond close ties. That is the one defensible form of “radius of trust.”
SourcesOECD (2017) Guidelines on measuring trust, pp. 41–44, 66–79; World Bank (2026) WGI; Afrobarometer (Mauritius 2020, Botswana 2021); WVS Association (2024). RES-002 evidence pack.
15 · Trust radius
Section 4 · Networks Classroom poll · static alternative

Close networks can hold cooperation together — and also hold it back.

Theory tested
Radius of Trust
Imagine a thousand dollars that must travel through other people before it reaches someone who will use it productively. Who do you hand it to first? The question is not academic. It is the start of a market: trust pays the frictions. The same close tie that lowers cost inside a group can also raise the cost of letting the outside in.
Anonymous classroom poll

Who would you trust with the money?

Choose only one. There is no correct answer. Your choice is read as a position, not an identity.

  1. A family member.
  2. A member of the same ethnic or religious community.
  3. A classmate you have known for less than a year.
  4. A stranger with a signed contract.
  5. A business association that will enforce rules on all members.
  6. A government agency with public accounts.

Static form of the group discussion GAME-002 (deferred). Running the live poll requires a platform/operator/time/ethics plan, a sample-size disclosure, and the explicit note that the result is a classroom reading — not a population estimate.

Support for cooperation Close ties lower the cost of enforcement when every actor knows every other actor. They explain small-firm trading networks, remittances, and informal credit — observed in all six cases.
Boundary against the outside The same tie can slow hiring across groups, raise the cost of outside capital, and keep firms below efficient scale. “Collectivism” is not low trust; it is bounded trust.
Institutional substitute Business associations, courts and public agencies can extend cooperation when close ties cannot. Mauritius’ EPZ worked because an export-processing authority adjudicated conflicts far outside family networks.
What we do not say No case here supports the strong claim that “generalized trust causes firm scale.” The project cell for Ghana and Korea is marked UNRESOLVED on this question.
SourcesOECD (2017); Nunn & Wantchekon (2011) AER, 101(7); Afrobarometer; Ipsos (Malaysia 2020). Discussion protocol: static alternative only; live interaction (GAME-002) deferred.

Section 4 · Mechanisms PDI & LTO as conditions · UAI/MAS/IVR as context

Authority and time horizons are conditional mechanisms — the other three are not.

Rule
If / then / but — not “national personality”
Power distance (PDI) — conditional mechanism
If authority is centralised and legitimated, then coordinated investment can be imposed on firms (export discipline, HCI push, land reform), but if authority is captured or unaccountable, the same centralisation produces mis-allocation and rent extraction. Korea’s HCI push works. The Philippines’ Marcos-era centralisation does not. Same dimension; different conditions.
Long-term orientation (LTO) — conditional mechanism
If firms, households and the state have the fiscal space, information and incentives to think across cycles, then patient investment and savings follow, but if credit markets are shallow, inflation is high, or political survival is short-term, long-run plans are rational to reject. “Culture” does not override a 1980s debt crisis.
Evidence insufficient

Uncertainty avoidance (UAI)

Not a measure of risk aversion (Hofstede, 2011, pp. 9–10). No matched mechanism to development outcomes is registered in the approved claims.

Evidence insufficient

Masculinity / femininity (MAS)

Section 5 · Test 1 of 3 Connected Development · supported with exceptions

Test 1 · Connected Development SUPPORTED-WITH-EXCEPTIONS

Question
Not how much infrastructure existed — but whom it connected.
Keep only the “productive network depth” version: economies grow where infrastructure, firms, learning and markets are joined into deep productive networks. The claim fails if large income gains occur without productive networks — and also fails if deep network connection coexists with shallow upgrading.
DEEP PRODUCTIVE NETWORK — SCHEMATIC

Many short, redundant links between firms, suppliers, workers and markets. Learning accumulates. Shocks spread through alternatives.

SHALLOW HUB — SCHEMATIC

Dense value at one node (a mine, a port, a multinational assembly line). High export share possible with limited domestic learning.

Question
How far beyond close networks can economic trust travel?
The strong version — generalized trust causes firm scale — is rejected. The revised version — institutions and organisations can plausibly substitute for low generalized trust — is defensible but the project has no matched historical six-country trust evidence to test it decisively. 20 · Test 2
Section 5 · Test 3 of 3 Continuity + Adaptation · supported with exceptions

Test 3 · Continuity + Adaptation SUPPORTED-WITH-EXCEPTIONS

Question
Can a country learn industrially if direction changes every few years?
Durable direction matters only when paired with performance feedback, correction and capability building. Continuity without discipline produces entrenched failure; policy churn without durable direction prevents learning. The verdict is supported with exceptions, not supported.
South Korea · strongest support · durable export discipline with mid-course correction
Mauritius · strong qualified support · deliberate sector-by-sector adaptation
Philippines · continuity-without-discipline warning
Botswana · accumulation-vs-diversification warning · the strongest challenge
The exception that teaches Botswana shows that durable direction (prudent mineral stewardship for sixty years) can produce high incomes without diversification. Continuity is not enough; adaptation into new sectors is also required if the aim is manufacturing deepening. Direction without correction can accumulate failure, too — Philippines’ long bureaucratic continuity did not deliver Korea-style upgrading.
SourcesWorld Bank (1993, 2020, 2023); Maipose (2008); Svirydzenka & Petri (2014); ADB (2007); Hill (2013); IMF (1998, 1999); Statistics Botswana (2021). THEORY-002 approved claims.

Section 6 · Synthesis Capability outcomes · multiple interacting forces

What the combined evidence actually says.

Form
Many forces, bounded confidence
Divergence depended less on fixed regional culture than on whether states, firms, workers and external partners built and disciplined productive capabilities over time. Culture may enter — through legitimacy, coordination, or organisational practice — but it must compete with non-cultural explanations. The constellation below is not a Venn diagram. It is a map of the forces whose interaction appears to matter, with each labelled by confidence and limitation.
Where confidence is highest Basic education and policy / discipline — supported across Korea, Malaysia, Mauritius; absent or incomplete elsewhere.
Where confidence is lower Infrastructure (Botswana exception), land & agriculture (case-specific), culture-as-mechanism (bounded to specific channels).
Where we remain silent No ranking of relative weights. The evidence does not permit a universal weighting; it supports a bounded, case-by-case interpretation.

23 · Synthesis
Section 6 · Takeaways What travels · what adapts · what we refuse to claim

What can travel, what must adapt, what we do not claim.

Rule of transfer
Capabilities travel · copying rarely does.
Six cases, three ideas, and a careful record of what the evidence can and cannot support. Here is the three-column takeaway. Each item is reduced to a short claim and a short reason.
TRAVEL
Basic education first Universal literacy is a precondition for firm learning in every supporting case. Expensive, slow, cumulative.
Policy with correction Durable direction only pays when paired with performance feedback. Korea’s HCI push was corrected; Marcos-era centralisation was not.
Institutional substitutes Business associations, courts, EPZ authorities can extend cooperation beyond close networks — the defensible “radius of trust.”
ADAPT
Export disciplines Credit tied to export performance worked in Korea’s context. Transplanting it needs a market, a currency regime, and an administration that can measure.
Mineral-revenue stewardship Botswana shows it is possible; it depends on a particular bargain with the mining company and a civil service capable of saving.
EPZ diversification Mauritius shows the path across sugar / textile / tourism / finance; it depended on market access the next country may not have.
DO NOT CLAIM
Culture as a single cause No cultural dimension alone identifies which case will grow. Hofstede dimensions are lenses, not fates.
Africa vs Asia Two blocs is not six cases. Both regions contain fast and slow growers.
Six-country trust ranking No matched evidence exists. “Trust rankings” based on WGI are not trust rankings.
If culture matters, can culture change?
The closing question is on page 25. It is not rhetorical: Korea’s policy environment, Mauritius’ sector mix, and Botswana’s stewardship each shifted across generations. The evidence we have supports the possibility that culture does change — because it already has.
