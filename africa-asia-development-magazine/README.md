# Africa & Asia: Diverging Development Paths

> **Restarting or assigning agents? Begin with [`START_HERE_AGENT_WORKFLOW.md`](START_HERE_AGENT_WORKFLOW.md).**

A research-led, magazine-style cultural analysis of why many African economies did not industrialise and grow in the same way as several East Asian economies.

The project combines economic development, colonial history, institutions and culture. It is designed as a visual investigation rather than a conventional essay. The magazine should invite readers to question explanations, make decisions, test ideas and compare cases before reaching a conclusion.

## Project purpose

The assignment asks the team to analyse economic development in Africa and explain why the region did not follow the same growth path as parts of Asia, with a cultural analysis at the centre of the work.

The project must avoid a simple claim that "African culture caused slow growth." Instead, it examines how culture may interact with colonial legacies, institutions, industrial policy, infrastructure, education, trust, trade and political continuity.

## Main question

> Why did many African and Asian economies follow different development paths, and how much can culture explain?

A recurring secondary question guides the magazine:

> Was slower growth caused by culture, or was culture itself partly shaped by history and institutions?

## Scope

- Compare selected countries and development paths rather than treating Africa or Asia as a single economy or culture.
- Use South Korea and Ghana as the main paired comparison.
- Use Botswana as a counterexample to simple culture-first explanations.
- Select one additional African case after research.
- Show variation within Asia as well as within Africa.
- Cover colonial institutions, borders, infrastructure and export structures.
- Use all six Hofstede dimensions, but give more space to the dimensions that are most relevant.
- Develop and test three original group theories.
- Separate verified evidence from interpretation, hypothesis and speculation.

## Main deliverable

The final deliverable is a magazine of approximately 25 pages, with slight variation allowed for references, credits, and front or back matter. It includes:

- sourced research and analysis;
- maps, charts, timelines and comparisons;
- country cases and counterexamples;
- original group theories presented as questions;
- cultural frameworks, including Hofstede;
- interactive elements such as polls, games and QR-linked content;
- full references and image credits;
- material that can support a class presentation by any team member.

The current page plan targets approximately 25 A4 pages. The existing 45-page PDF is a legacy storyboard and idea bank, not the current pagination target.

## Key themes

1. Structural transformation and productivity
2. Colonial rule and inherited institutions
3. Borders, infrastructure and market connection
4. Manufacturing, exports and industrial learning
5. Trust, authority and long-term orientation
6. Cultural frameworks and their limits
7. Counterexamples and theory testing
8. Lessons that can be adapted rather than copied

## Original group theories

The team currently plans to test three working theories:

1. **Connected Development Theory** - The important issue may not be how much infrastructure exists, but whom and what it connects.
2. **Radius of Trust Theory** - Firms and formal markets may scale more easily when trust reaches beyond family and close networks into institutions and professional relationships.
3. **Continuity + Adaptation Theory** - Industrial capability may require policy direction that lasts long enough for learning, while still allowing failed policies to be corrected.

These are working hypotheses created for the project. They are not presented as established academic theories.

## How the project is being developed

The project follows a simple research cycle:

1. Observe a difference or repeated pattern.
2. Connect economic, historical, institutional and cultural explanations.
3. State a prediction.
4. Test it with data and cases.
5. Search for counterexamples.
6. Revise the explanation when it fails.

The magazine uses questions before conclusions. Readers should encounter a problem, make a choice or form an opinion before the analysis is revealed.
