# Research Overview

**Project:** Africa & Asia — Diverging Development Paths
**Format:** Research-led magazine (~25 pages)
**Date:** 2026-10-12

---

## The main question

> Why did many African and Asian economies follow different development paths, and how much can culture explain?

A secondary question runs underneath the whole magazine:

> Was slower growth caused by culture, or was culture itself partly shaped by history and institutions?

The project does **not** claim that "African culture caused slow growth." It treats culture as one interacting factor alongside colonial legacies, institutions, industrial policy, infrastructure, education, trust, trade, and political continuity.

---

## How the research is structured

### Six matched countries

| Region | Countries |
|---|---|
| Africa | Ghana, Botswana, Mauritius |
| Asia | South Korea, Malaysia, Philippines |

Three comparison years are locked: **1960, 1990, 2020**. Each country has a verified evidence file covering GDP per capita, labour productivity, employment by sector, manufacturing share, exports, literacy, urbanisation, electricity, firm-size/informality (where comparable), and an institutional/trust measure. Every value is tied to a registered source.

### The paired comparison

- **Ghana vs. South Korea** — the central matched case: similar GDP per capita in 1960, very different trajectories by 2020.
- **Botswana** — an African counterexample to simple culture-first explanations.
- **Mauritius, Malaysia, Philippines** — show variation *within* each region, so neither continent is treated as one culture or one economy.

## The three original theories

These are **working hypotheses created by the team**, not established academic theories.

### 1. Connected Development Theory
> The decisive issue may not be *how much* infrastructure exists, but *whom and what* it connects.

Infrastructure that only moves raw materials from mine to port may increase exports without building a domestic industrial network. Infrastructure that connects firms, workers, and cities enables productivity to spread.

**Verdict after testing:** Accepted in a "productive-network-depth" form. Botswana challenges the idea that inherited infrastructure is a prerequisite; the Philippines challenges the idea that connection is sufficient.

### 2. Radius of Trust Theory
> Firms and formal markets may scale more easily when trust reaches beyond family and close networks into institutions and professional relationships.

**Verdict after testing:** Inconclusive in its "institutional / organisational substitutes" form. Different trust constructs (personal, network, named-institution, governance, survey) are kept separate and are not combined into one scale.

### 3. Continuity + Adaptation Theory
> Industrial capability may require policy direction that lasts long enough for learning, while still correcting failed policies.

**Verdict after testing:** Accepted with exceptions. Botswana is treated as an accumulation/diversification warning rather than a direct test of duration + feedback.

A final theory-evidence matrix brings all three together: **55 approved claims, 44 sources, 181 claim groups, 512 valid claim-to-source pairings.**

## Culture (Hofstede) — used with critical rules

All six Hofstede dimensions are included, but the magazine applies four critical-use rules:

1. **Ecological fallacy warning** — country scores are not individual personalities.
2. **Within-country variation** — single scores hide major internal differences.
3. **Measurement and time-stability caveats** — scores are not fixed laws of a nation.
4. **No causal overreach** — a correlation with growth is not a cause of growth.

Three dimensions are used as **conditional mechanisms** only, tied to direct institutional or policy evidence:

- Individualism / collectivism → in-group / out-group behaviour
- Power distance → voice and feedback inside institutions
- Long-term orientation → investment and learning questions

The other three dimensions (uncertainty avoidance, masculinity/femininity, indulgence/restraint) are **excluded** from the main theory on current evidence, with the conditions that would reopen them recorded.

## What the evidence can show, and what it cannot

### Shows clearly

- Starting positions in 1960 were much closer than people usually assume.
- By 1990 and 2020, the six countries diverge sharply on GDP per capita, employment structure, and manufacturing share.
- Variation **within** Africa and **within** Asia is as large as variation between the regions.
- Colonial inheritance, institutions, and policy continuity each explain more than culture alone.

### Cannot prove

- That any single factor (culture, colonialism, geography, policy) caused the divergence on its own.
- A single six-country export-composition chart — the classifications are not compatible, so exports are handled as narrative evidence.
- Direct cross-country comparisons of personal trust, firm size, or informality on one scale.
- A seamless Philippines manufacturing trajectory across all three years.

Every chart in the magazine carries a visible "What this cannot prove" note where causality might be over-read.

## Research method

The project follows a short, visible cycle on every claim:

1. Observe a difference or repeated pattern.
2. Connect economic, historical, institutional, and cultural explanations.
3. State a prediction.
4. Test it with data and matched cases.
5. Search actively for counterexamples.
6. Revise the explanation when the counterexample wins.

The magazine deliberately puts **questions before conclusions**: readers are asked to make a choice or form an opinion before the evidence is revealed.

---

## Source and citation standards

- APA 7 throughout.
- A shared source register (97 sources) and a claim register (292 claims) with zero dangling links.
- Each registered claim is tied to its exact locator (page, table, figure, chapter, or indicator).
- Every final chart has a data file, an APA figure note, and a comparability check.
- The old 45-page storyboard PDF is explicitly **not** treated as evidence — its figures and maps are placeholders unless re-verified in the register.

---

## What this magazine is trying to be

Not an essay. Not a single explanation. A **visual investigation** that:

- compares countries fairly on matched definitions, years, and axes;
- separates verified evidence from hypothesis and speculation;
- shows readers the counterexamples, not just the supporting cases;
- treats culture as one interacting factor rather than a verdict on any society.

If the reader finishes the magazine less certain of simple explanations and more able to see how history, institutions, and culture *interact*, the project has done its job.
